Baking Science & Fermentation

The Science of Wood-Fired Sourdough Fermentation and Hearth Baking

By Julian Vance • September 2026 • 10 Min Read • Verified Culinary Research

1. The Microbial Ecology of Spontaneous Sourdough Starters

At the heart of every magnificent evening supper sits a loaf of freshly baked hearth bread. Unlike commercial industrial breads leavened with isolated mono-cultures of commercial dry yeast, authentic sourdough fermentation represents a dynamic, biodiverse ecosystem of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria (LAB). When stone-milled whole grains are mixed with non-chlorinated spring water and left exposed to ambient air, dormant wild microbes inhabiting the grain hulls awaken into active metabolic life.

In our Mercer Street baking atelier, our sourdough culture has been continuously nurtured and propagated for decades. The dominant microbial partners in this symbiotic community are Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis and wild yeasts such as Kazachstania exigua. These two micro-organisms have evolved an exquisite metabolic harmony: the bacteria cannot metabolize maltose sugars, which the wild yeast thrives upon, while the yeast does not consume the simple glucose fragments released by the bacteria. This cooperative coexistence produces the signature complex flavor, tender crumb structure, and superior digestibility that defines genuine hearth baking.

2. Lactic Acid vs. Acetic Acid Enzymatic Dynamics

The characteristic tang of artisanal sourdough is governed by the precise ratio of two organic acids produced during metabolic fermentation: lactic acid and acetic acid. Lactic acid (produced predominantly by homofermentative lactobacilli in warmer, wetter dough environments) imparts a soft, creamy, yogurty acidity. Acetic acid (produced by heterofermentative bacteria in cooler, firmer, long-fermented doughs) delivers a sharp, vinegar-like punch.

For an evening supper banquet, an overly sour bread can overpower delicate roasted meats or subtle vegetable broths. We calibrate our dough hydrations to a high 78 percent and enforce a slow 24-hour cold retard proofing cycle at exactly 4°C (39°F). This temperature window slows gas production by the wild yeasts while allowing lactic acid bacteria to continuously synthesize enzymes that break down complex grain phytates and gluten proteins, producing a bread with subtle, nutty sweetness and gentle digestive ease.

3. Wood-Fired Hearth Thermodynamics and High-Steam Baking

Transferring a proofed loaf into a wood-fired stone hearth oven involves dramatic thermodynamic transfers. In modern domestic ovens, heat is primarily conductive and convective, often drying out the surface of the loaf before the interior has fully expanded. In our wood-fired brick oven, the thermal mass of three tons of refractory firebrick radiates steady infrared energy from every direction.

When the loaf hits the 800°F stone hearth floor, trapped water inside the dough vaporizes rapidly, generating sudden internal pressure known as "oven spring." To prevent the crust from hardening prematurely and trapping the expanding loaf, we introduce high-pressure water steam into the baking chamber during the initial ten minutes. The steam condenses onto the cool dough surface, keeping the starch gelled and supple while gelatinizing surface starches into the glassy, blistered, deeply caramelized crust that is the hallmark of true artisan baking.

4. The Maillard Reaction and Starch Dextrinization in Crust Formation

As the steam dissipates and the oven temperature stabilizes around 450°F (232°C), the exterior crust undergoes two simultaneous biochemical transformations: the Maillard reaction and caramelization. The Maillard reaction—a chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars—produces hundreds of flavor compounds, including pyrazines, furans, and pyrroles, which impart aromas of roasted hazelnut, malt, and toasted coffee.

Simultaneously, surface starches break down under dry radiant heat into simpler dextrins. These dextrin molecules fuse together into a thin, crispy, glass-like shell that crackles audibly when the loaf is retrieved from the oven with a wooden peel. When sliced and served warm at the communal table with hand-churned grass-fed butter, this hearth bread sets the emotional and sensory tone for the entire banquet.
